> This is a personal reflection, not medical advice or a substitute for professional care. Depression is treatable, but recovery is different for everyone. Please follow your clinician's advice about medication and treatment.

- One part of recovering from depression can be working through what feels unresolved. People sometimes say that long experience makes you an expert. After living with depression for ten years, I have my own perspective, and I want to record some of my feelings and the thoughts I had along the way.
- Medication and other treatments can help with symptoms, while recovery may also involve understanding what is affecting you and finding support for the difficulties underneath. For me, that included trying to make sense of the things I felt stuck on.
- What do I mean by feeling stuck? It might be regret, something you wish you could change, or pain that feels impossible to escape. At times it can feel like a nightmare you cannot wake from. Even if someone else cannot see it, that distress is real to the person experiencing it. Finding a way forward can be hard, especially when familiar ways of thinking, routines, behaviors, and circumstances reinforce one another. You do not have to work through that alone.
- In my own experience, making changes to my routines and habits, and finding support to change difficult circumstances, helped me begin to move forward. One practice that mattered to me was making room for love: caring for myself, the people around me, and the world. I started by practicing words of kindness, even silently. I imagined moments in which I could express care and pictured a future in which I felt connected to the world and to other people. When I went outside, I tried to notice the sky and the light, to enjoy the warmth of the sun, and to appreciate flowers, grass, passersby, and the people traveling by. I wished them well. This was a personal practice that brought me comfort; it may not be right for everyone, and it is not a replacement for treatment.
- With time and support, I began to feel more like myself and less defined by depression. I still take my clinician's guidance seriously: if medication is part of your treatment, do not stop or change it without speaking with your prescriber. I remind myself that I have experienced depression and that I can be attentive to my well-being without blaming or fearing myself. I try to treat difficult feelings as signals to pause, seek support, and consider whether something in my environment or routine needs attention. I am a person, not a diagnosis, and I deserve care.
- Moving forward, for me, meant changing some habits and circumstances and finding new ways to understand the world. Those changes also affected my habits and circumstances in return.
- So, when you feel ready, take a moment to look at the world around you with a little tenderness.
- And perhaps rest. Think about how long it has been since you had a proper break. You may be exhausted, and you deserve time to sleep and recover.
- When you wake, try to meet the real person you are in your real life with patience.
- You do not have to be perfect. The world is imperfect, too. I have come to feel that living honestly matters more than getting everything right.
- You may find that you want to help make the world kinder, better, and more loving.
- So, when you feel ready, take a moment to look at the world around you with a little tenderness.

- If you still feel unable to get through this, please reach out to someone now: a trusted person, a mental-health professional, or a local crisis service. If you might hurt yourself or cannot stay safe, contact your local emergency services or go to an emergency department, and ask someone you trust to stay with you. You deserve help, and you do not have to wait alone for things to change.
- I often hear people wonder: Is there really a God? Where is God? I have wondered about this, too. One answer that came to me was: “First, I’m glad you’ve begun asking the question. What comes next is yours to discover.” Whether or not you believe in God, you can look for whatever brings you comfort, connection, and hope.
- When pain feels overwhelming, it can be hard to imagine that anything will change. Please do not treat death as a solution or face those thoughts by yourself. Tell someone you trust, contact a mental-health professional or crisis service, and seek emergency help if you may be in immediate danger. You are not alone: many people have faced deep suffering, and support and treatment can help people find a way through. You do not have to solve everything right now. For this moment, focus on staying safe and letting someone be with you.
- END - FRIEND: Even as friends, we cannot escape the “end.” I hope we can be the best of friends without needing to meet, because we were once comrades: we faced hardship side by side and made it through together.
- When you feel better, perhaps that will be the time for us to say goodbye. Everyone is busy, and we all have lives to enjoy. Share the care you have in your heart, and help make it more likely that your family, friends, and colleagues can live healthy and peaceful lives.
- A teacher once said: “Illness does not mean you did something wrong or that heaven is punishing you. Sometimes we have spent too long neglecting ourselves—keeping our bodies tense, working beyond our limits, and holding in sadness and hurt. Over time, exhaustion and distress can build up, until one day we become ill.”
- Forgive others as you hope to be forgiven, and be grateful when others show you kindness. Give people the chance to return the kindness you have shown them.
- Everything you have lived through is part of your story. You are unique, and you have the right to pursue the life you want.
- As a friend, there is still so much I would like to say. But it is time to say goodbye. I hope we meet again. For now...
- I wish you healing, peace, and good health.
